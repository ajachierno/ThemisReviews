Pros and cons by protocol

Z-Wave

908.42 MHz (US), 868.42 MHz (EU)

Low-power mesh on a sub-GHz band, built for home automation and nothing else.

Our take: The safest pick for a hub-based setup that has to keep working for years. Every certified device talks to every certified controller, and its band stays out of Wi-Fi's way.

Pros

  • Sub-GHz radio doesn't compete with Wi-Fi or Zigbee on 2.4 GHz
  • Lower frequency gets through walls and floors better than 2.4 GHz
  • Mandatory certification, so devices from different brands work together
  • Every mains-powered device repeats the signal and extends the mesh
  • S2 security is required on newer certified devices
  • Z-Wave Long Range reaches outbuildings and supports up to 4,000 devices

Cons

  • Needs a hub or USB controller; there's no phone-only setup
  • Devices usually cost more than Zigbee or Wi-Fi equivalents
  • Frequencies are regional, so a US device won't work on an EU controller
  • Low bandwidth rules out cameras and anything streaming
  • Classic mesh tops out at 232 devices and 4 hops

Zigbee

2.4 GHz (IEEE 802.15.4)

Cheap, low-power 2.4 GHz mesh with the widest catalog of small sensors.

Our take: The budget king for sensors and bulbs. Pair it with a good coordinator and plenty of plug-in repeaters and it's rock solid.

Pros

  • Sensors and buttons are among the cheapest on the market
  • Huge catalog from Aqara, IKEA, Philips Hue, Sonoff, Third Reality, and others
  • Mains-powered devices act as routers and strengthen the mesh
  • Coin-cell battery devices run for a year or more
  • Works fully local with Home Assistant (ZHA or Zigbee2MQTT)

Cons

  • Shares the 2.4 GHz band with Wi-Fi, so channel planning matters
  • Some brands bend the spec; Aqara devices can drop off third-party routers
  • Needs a coordinator (hub or USB stick)
  • Brand hubs like Hue only accept their own approved devices
  • Bulbs used as repeaters break the mesh when someone flips the wall switch

Wi-Fi

2.4 GHz (most smart devices), 5 GHz (cameras)

Uses the router you already own. No hub, lots of bandwidth, lots of power draw.

Our take: Right for cameras, doorbells, and anything plugged in. Wrong for battery sensors, and a few dozen devices can overload a cheap router.

Pros

  • No hub; it joins the network you already have
  • Enough bandwidth for live video and audio
  • Widest choice of brands and the lowest entry prices
  • Setup is usually an app and a few taps

Cons

  • Power hungry; battery Wi-Fi sensors need frequent charging
  • Many devices only support 2.4 GHz, which complicates setup on mixed-band routers
  • Dozens of devices can overload consumer routers
  • Many products depend on the maker's cloud and stop working if it shuts down
  • No mesh between devices; range is whatever your router covers

Bluetooth

2.4 GHz

Phone-to-device and short range. Easy to set up, hard to reach from outside the room.

Our take: Fine for one-room gadgets you control from your phone. For whole-house automation, plan on a hub or Bluetooth proxies.

Pros

  • Pairs straight to your phone with no hub or network
  • Bluetooth LE sips power, so sensors run for months or years
  • Keeps working when the internet is down
  • Home Assistant can extend coverage with cheap ESP32 Bluetooth proxies

Cons

  • Short range, often a single room through walls
  • No remote control unless a hub, bridge, or proxy is in range
  • Bluetooth Mesh has little product support for home automation
  • Many devices work only through the maker's own app

Thread

2.4 GHz (IEEE 802.15.4)

IP-based, self-healing 2.4 GHz mesh. The low-power radio behind most new Matter devices.

Our take: The best bet for new battery devices if you already own a border router. The catalog is still smaller than Zigbee's or Z-Wave's.

Pros

  • Every device gets an IPv6 address, so there's no proprietary translation layer
  • Self-healing mesh with no single point of failure if you run more than one border router
  • Low power with fast response times
  • Border routers are built into devices many people already own: HomePod mini, Apple TV 4K, Nest Hub (2nd gen), and some Echo models

Cons

  • Needs a Thread border router
  • Border routers from different ecosystems don't always share one network
  • Smaller product catalog than Zigbee or Z-Wave
  • Shares the 2.4 GHz band with Wi-Fi and Zigbee
  • Newer standard, so firmware and pairing bugs still show up

Matter

Wi-Fi, Thread, or Ethernet

A shared language, not a radio. Runs over Wi-Fi, Thread, or Ethernet and works across Apple, Google, Amazon, SmartThings, and Home Assistant.

Our take: Buy Matter devices when you can, because they keep your options open. Check that the features you need are in the spec before you buy.

Pros

  • One device works with Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, SmartThings, and Home Assistant
  • Multi-admin lets several ecosystems control the same device at once
  • Local control by design, no cloud needed for day-to-day use
  • Setup is a QR code scan in whichever app you prefer

Cons

  • Only device types in the current spec are supported, so advanced features often stay in the maker's app
  • Each platform exposes a different slice of what a device can do
  • Pairing and multi-admin bugs are still common
  • Matter over Thread devices still need a Thread border router

Lutron Clear Connect

434 MHz (Clear Connect Type A)

Lutron's own RF used by Caseta and RadioRA. Proprietary and very reliable.

Our take: If you only care about lighting and want it to just work, this is the benchmark. Expect to stay inside the Lutron catalog.

Pros

  • Very reliable; its band has almost no competing traffic
  • Dimmers work with a wide range of LED bulbs without flicker
  • Pico remotes run for years and need no wiring
  • Integrates with HomeKit, Alexa, Google, SmartThings, and Home Assistant through the bridge

Cons

  • Only Lutron devices join the network
  • Requires a Lutron bridge or processor
  • Lighting and shades only; no general-purpose sensors
  • Costs more per switch than most Zigbee or Wi-Fi options

433 MHz RF

433.92 MHz (some US gear uses 315 MHz)

Simple one-way radio found in cheap sensors, weather stations, and remotes.

Our take: Great for cheap outdoor sensors you only need to read. Skip it for anything security-related.

Pros

  • Very cheap sensors
  • Long range through walls and out to the yard
  • Batteries last a long time
  • A $30 SDR dongle running rtl_433 can read hundreds of device models

Cons

  • No common standard; each brand uses its own encoding
  • Mostly one-way, with no confirmation that a command arrived
  • Typically no encryption, so signals can be sniffed or replayed
  • Needs an RF bridge or SDR to get into a smart home system
